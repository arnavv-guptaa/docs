Mean reversion is the tendency of many financial series to drift back toward their long-run average after straying far from it. Currencies, valuations, sentiment and volatility all show it to some degree.

## Beginner

### What It Means

Some things in markets behave like a stretched rubber band: the further they get pulled from normal, the stronger the pull back. A currency far above its historical range, a sentiment reading at a euphoric extreme, a volatility spike, all tend, on average, to move back toward their typical levels over time.

### Example

A currency that has rallied far above its long-run average is performing well right now. Mean-reversion logic does not deny that. It asks a different question: from this stretched level, is the path forward more likely to continue up or to normalize down? History usually answers: the further from normal, the more the odds tilt toward normalization.

### Why It Matters

Mean reversion is the discipline that stops extrapolation. The natural instinct is to project recent performance forward; mean-reversion logic asks whether the recent move has already carried the asset to a level from which history says the path forward is tilted the other way.

## Advanced

### How to Read It

The reports invoke mean reversion mainly where it is best documented, currencies and sentiment, and almost always in the language of [z-scores](/glossary/z-score): "stretched", "extended", "room to normalize". It is a probabilistic statement, not a timing call: extremes can persist, but the odds shift against them the further they stretch.

### Common Misreadings

- **Applying it everywhere**: not all series mean-revert. Equity index levels, for example, trend upward over long horizons, and "reversion" logic applied to them is a losing framework; the property belongs to bounded or anchored series like valuation ratios, real exchange rates, and sentiment
- **Using it as a timer**: mean reversion says which direction the odds lean, never when the move comes; stretched can become more stretched first
- **Confusing it with contrarianism**: mean reversion is a statistical property of a series; contrarianism is a stance, the reports use the former to calibrate expectations, not to reflexively fade every move

### The Tension With Momentum

Mean reversion and [momentum](/glossary/momentum) coexist because they operate on different horizons and different objects: momentum is strongest over intermediate horizons in relative asset returns, while mean reversion dominates at extremes and over longer horizons in anchored series. The reports respect both, riding confirmed trends while flagging when a series has stretched to levels from which history urges caution.
